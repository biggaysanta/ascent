# Pain at the Base of the Skull and Neck: Suboccipital Tension

## The Suboccipital Triangle and Cranio-Cervical Dynamics

Pain concentrated at the base of the skull, transitioning into the upper cervical spine, represents a specific structural dysfunction localized around the occiput, atlas (C1), and axis (C2). This anatomical junction is controlled by the suboccipital muscle group: the rectus capitis posterior major, rectus capitis posterior minor, obliquus capitis superior, and obliquus capitis inferior. 

These deep, highly specialized muscles possess an extraordinarily high density of muscle spindles, which feed positional data directly to the central nervous system to coordinate visual and vestibular tracking. When an individual adopts a chronic forward-head posture—common during long shifts driving on Sacramento highways or staring at non-ergonomic screens—the suboccipital muscles are forced into a state of chronic eccentric hypertonicity. They must contract continuously to prevent the skull from rolling forward off the cervical spine, leading to significant structural distress and localized tissue ischemia.

## Understanding Occipital Neuralgia

A primary consequence of chronic suboccipital hypertonicity is the mechanical entrapment of the Greater Occipital Nerve. This nerve originates from the dorsal ramus of the C2 spinal nerve, exits below the obliquus capitis inferior, and pierces through the belly of the semispinalis capitis and upper trapezius to provide sensation to the posterior scalp.

When the suboccipital triangle becomes hypertonic and locked, the Greater Occipital Nerve suffers direct compression. This triggers a distinct neurological condition known as occipital neuralgia. Symptoms include a deep, throbbing, or shooting pain that starts precisely at the base of the skull and projects forward over the cranium, often settling directly behind one eye. This pathway is frequently misdiagnosed as a standard migraine, but its source is entirely mechanical and musculoskeletal within the upper neck.

Muscular trigger points within the suboccipital group refer pain in a classic 'ghost headache' pattern:


Alleviating pain at the base of the skull requires precise anatomical decompression rather than global, aggressive traction.
