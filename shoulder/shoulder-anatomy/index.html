The Anatomy of the Scapula: Bony Landmarks, Muscle Attachments, and Clinical Insights

July 9, 2026

TL;DR

  • The scapula serves as the structural anchor for the upper extremity, acting as the interface for exactly 17 distinct muscular attachments.
  • Key bony landmarks—including the spine, acromion, coracoid process, glenoid fossa, and the superior border’s suprascapular notch—serve as critical mechanical levers.
  • The inferior belly of the omohyoid attaches directly to the superior border, bridging scapular stability with hyoid and laryngeal function during deglutition and speech.
  • Scapular dyskinesis frequently presents as diffuse neck or shoulder tension, requiring precise anatomical mapping to resolve foundational mechanical faults.

Introduction: The Unsung Anchor of the Upper Extremity

The scapula, or shoulder blade, is a masterpiece of evolutionary engineering. Flat, triangular, and suspended entirely by muscular attachments against the posterior thoracic wall, it provides the structural foundation for nearly every movement of the upper limb. While patients frequently present with complaints of “mid-back tightness” or “shoulder pinches,” the root cause is often found in the intricate architecture of this bone.

Understanding the scapula requires more than memorizing names on a skeleton. It demands an appreciation for how its bony landmarks act as mechanical levers for muscles that coordinate everything from overhead lifting to postural stabilization. When these mechanics fail, the entire kinetic chain of the upper extremity suffers.

The Architecture: Key Bony Landmarks

The scapula features three borders (superior, lateral, medial), three angles (superior, inferior, lateral), and two main surfaces (costal and dorsal). Within this geometry lie the specific projections, depressions, and notches that dictate upper-limb and cervical function.

1. The Spine of the Scapula and the Acromion

Prominently traversing the dorsal surface is the spine of the scapula. You can easily palpate this subcutaneous ridge on almost any patient. Traveling laterally, the spine flattens and extends forward to form the acromion process.

  • The acromion articulates with the clavicle at the acromioclavicular (AC) joint, forming the roof of the subacromial space.
  • This space houses the supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa.
  • Structural variations in the acromion (flat, curved, or hooked) directly impact the prevalence of subacromial impingement syndrome.

2. The Coracoid Process and the Suprascapular Notch

Projecting anteriorly and laterally from the superior border is the coracoid process. Often described as a “bent finger” pointing toward the shoulder joint, this projection serves as a crucial anchor point for anterior stabilizing structures.

  • It counterbalances the posterior pull of the back musculature.
  • Immediately medial to the base of the coracoid process lies the suprascapular notch, a distinct depression along the superior border converted into a foramen by the superior transverse scapular ligament. This notch transmits the suprascapular nerve, a frequent site for nerve entrapment.

3. The Glenoid Fossa and Supraglenoid/Infraglenoid Tubercles

The lateral angle houses the glenoid fossa, a shallow, pear-shaped cavity that receives the head of the humerus to form the glenohumeral joint.

  • Because the glenoid is shallow (providing mobility at the expense of inherent stability), it relies on the fibrocartilaginous glenoid labrum and surrounding dynamic stabilizers.
  • Immediately superior and inferior to the cavity are the supraglenoid tubercle and infraglenoid tubercle, which serve as proximate attachments for the long heads of the biceps and triceps brachii, respectively.

4. The Fossa System: Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, and Subscapular

The spine divides the dorsal surface into two unequal segments: the superior, smaller supraspinous fossa and the inferior, larger infraspinous fossa. The entire anterior or costal surface forms a broad, concave depression known as the subscapular fossa, which glides smoothly over the rib cage via the serratus anterior interface.

Muscular Interfaces: The Full 17 Attachments

Exactly seventeen muscles attach to or originate from a single scapula. This high density of muscular interfaces turns the bone into a dynamic tug-of-war station where balance is everything.
